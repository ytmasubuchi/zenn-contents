# IEEE 754 and Bit-Exact Reproducibility of Floating-Point Computation

## Which operations does IEEE 754-2019 require to be correctly rounded?

### Takeaway
IEEE 754-2019 requires exact, single-rounding ("correctly rounded") results only for a small, closed set of operations: the basic arithmetic operations (add, subtract, multiply, divide), square root, fused multiply-add (FMA), remainder, and format conversions. Everything outside this set — notably the transcendental/elementary functions (exp, log, sin, cos, pow, etc.) — is explicitly *not* required to be correctly rounded; it is only "recommended" under clause 9.2.

### Cited Findings
- IEEE 754-2019's general rounding requirement: "Except where stated otherwise, every operation shall be performed as if it first produced an intermediate result correct to infinite precision and with unbounded range, and then rounded that result according to one of the attributes in [the standard]." — [IEEE Standard 754-2019 PDF](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~vinals/tspot_files/phys4041/2020/IEEE%20Standard%20754-2019.pdf) (accessed 2026-10-14; text located via search of the document, full normative text not independently re-extractable from the PDF's internal content streams in this session — see Gaps)
- "According to IEEE 754-2019 Sections 5.4.1 and 5.5.1, the mandatory operations include: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, square root, and fused multiply-add. These are considered the basic computational operations that must be implemented by any conformant system." — search-synthesized summary of [IEEE Standard 754-2019 PDF](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~vinals/tspot_files/phys4041/2020/IEEE%20Standard%20754-2019.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14
- The FMA definition: "fusedMultiplyAdd(x, y, z) computes (x × y) + z as if with unbounded range and precision, rounding only once to the destination format." — [IEEE Standard 754-2019 PDF](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~vinals/tspot_files/phys4041/2020/IEEE%20Standard%20754-2019.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14. This single-rounding property is precisely what distinguishes FMA from a separate multiply-then-add (which rounds twice) and is the reason FMA fusion changes bit-level results (see compiler-flags section below).
- "Correct rounding is defined as the standard's method of converting an infinitely precise result to a floating-point number as determined by the applicable rounding direction, with a floating-point number so obtained being said to be correctly rounded." — synthesized from [IEEE Standard 754-2019 PDF](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~vinals/tspot_files/phys4041/2020/IEEE%20Standard%20754-2019.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14
- Conversions between supported formats (including different radices, e.g., decimal string ↔ binary) are required to be correctly rounded and to correctly signal the inexact and other exceptions. — [Differences Among IEEE 754 Implementations (IEEE background addendum)](https://grouper.ieee.org/groups/msc/ANSI_IEEE-Std-754-2019/background/addendum.html), accessed 2026-10-14, and corroborated by search synthesis of the 754-2019 text
- The `formatOf` naming convention in the standard indicates that an operation's destination format can be specified independently of its operands' format (relevant to general-computational "formatOf" conversions and mixed-format arithmetic). — [grouper.ieee.org background materials](https://grouper.ieee.org/groups/msc/ANSI_IEEE-Std-754-2019/background/addendum.html) / search synthesis, accessed 2026-10-14
- Even for a required, correctly-rounded operation, IEEE 754-1985/2019 ties "correct rounding" to the *destination's format*, and that destination format can be system- or user-supplied; per the IEEE background document: "the IEEE standard requires that each result be rounded correctly to the precision of the destination into which it will be placed," while noting that determination of the destination itself is not fully constrained. — [Differences Among IEEE 754 Implementations](https://grouper.ieee.org/groups/msc/ANSI_IEEE-Std-754-2019/background/addendum.html), accessed 2026-10-14

### Inferences
- Because these required operations are defined as "round(exact mathematical result)" with a uniquely defined mathematical result and a uniquely defined rounding function for a given rounding attribute, two independent, standard-conformant implementations of **any one** of these operations, given bit-identical inputs and the same rounding-direction attribute, are logically forced to produce the bit-identical output — there is no implementation freedom left once format, inputs, and rounding mode are fixed. This is the real locus of "IEEE 754 guarantees bit-exactness," and it applies strictly at the level of a single operation, not a program (see the dedicated question below on bit-exactness conditions).

### Gaps
- I could not mechanically re-extract verbatim clause/subclause text (e.g. exact wording of 5.1, 5.4.1, 5.4.2, 5.4.3, 5.6.1) directly from the IEEE 754-2019 PDF in this session — the PDF's text layer did not extract cleanly through the fetch tool used, so subclause numbers above (5.4.1, 5.5.1) are reported as given by a secondary AI-assisted summary of the document rather than independently verified by direct quotation. The standard itself is paywalled by IEEE/techstreet; treat exact subclause numbers as "likely correct, not independently re-verified character-for-character" and cross-check against the full standard text or a university course PDF mirror before publishing a claim that depends on the exact clause number.

## Clause 9.2 "recommended operations" — what is it, and why only recommended?

### Takeaway
Clause 9.2 of IEEE 754-2019 lists transcendental/elementary functions (exp, log, sin, cos, pow, etc.) as *recommended*, not required, operations. The reason is the "Table Maker's Dilemma": unlike the arithmetic primitives, there is no known general algorithm or proof bounding how many extra digits of precision are needed to correctly round an arbitrary transcendental function at an arbitrary point, so guaranteeing correct rounding for these functions is far more expensive and, in the general case, not provably decidable in bounded time — hence the standard recommends, rather than mandates, correct rounding for them. In practice, essentially no mainstream C/C++ libm correctly rounds all of exp/log/sin/pow, so cross-library and cross-architecture results for these functions routinely differ at the last bit or few bits.

### Cited Findings
- "IEEE 754-2019 Section 9.2 describes 'recommended' (not required) transcendental floating-point operations. The standard explicitly does not mandate a particular correctly-rounded algorithm for these operations (unlike required ops such as sqrt)." — search synthesis of [IEEE Standard 754-2019 PDF](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~vinals/tspot_files/phys4041/2020/IEEE%20Standard%20754-2019.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14
- "Section 9.2 includes unary operations like fp.exp, fp.log, fp.sin, fp.cos, and fp.tan, among other transcendental and trigonometric functions." — same source as above, accessed 2026-10-14; corroborated independently by [Z3Prover PR #10941 "Add IEEE 754-2019 recommended transcendental floating-point operators"](https://github.com/Z3Prover/z3/pull/10941), accessed 2026-10-14, which implements exactly this clause's operator set in an SMT solver's floating-point theory
- "A conforming operation shall return results correctly rounded for the applicable rounding direction for all operands in its domain" — this is the content of the *recommendation* itself: IF an implementation chooses to provide one of these operations as a conformant 9.2 operation, it *should* be correctly rounded, but providing the operation at all, and providing it correctly rounded, are both optional. "Transcendental, hyperbolic and trigonometric functions are labeled as 'recommended', and an IEEE 754 conformant programming language may not include functions such as the exponential or sine functions... no C/C++ implementation is known to comply with the recommendation that such functions be correctly rounded." — search synthesis of [IEEE Standard 754-2019 PDF](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~vinals/tspot_files/phys4041/2020/IEEE%20Standard%20754-2019.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14
- The "Table Maker's Dilemma" (TMD), the technical reason correct rounding is only recommended: "the hardness of obtaining correctly rounded results for elementary functions," coined by William Kahan. "In order to correctly choose the rounded nearest floating point number in approximate computations, one sometimes has to compute many extra bits of the result's significand... no proof exists to predict how many extra digits will have to be carried to compute a transcendental expression and round it correctly to some preassigned number of digits, and even the fact that a finite number of extra digits will ultimately suffice may be a deep theorem." — search synthesis citing [The Table Maker's Dilemma (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/2508776_The_Table_Maker's_Dilemma) and the [ANR Table Maker's Dilemma project page](https://anr.fr//Project-ANR-10-BLAN-0203), accessed 2026-10-14
- "The IEEE-754 standard requires correct rounding for fundamental arithmetic operations such as addition, multiplication and FMA, and this can be easily achieved by adding predetermined extra bits called guard bits that can be obtained using analytic proofs. However, such proofs do not exist for elementary functions such as the trigonometric, exponential and logarithmic functions. Correct rounding for these functions only became recommended in the last revision of the standard IEEE-754-2008." — search synthesis of [A Table Lookup-less Method for Correctly Rounded IEEE-754 Elementary Functions](http://eece.cu.edu.eg/~hfahmy/thesis/2015_10_elementary.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14
- Current research systems exist specifically to approximate the 9.2 ideal that the standard itself does not mandate, e.g. "RLIBM-32: High Performance Correctly Rounded Math Libraries for 32-bit Floating Point Representations" and "GPU-accelerated generation of correctly-rounded elementary functions" — [arXiv:2104.04043](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.04043) and [arXiv:1211.3056](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.3056), accessed 2026-10-14. Their existence as active research (rather than solved-and-shipped-everywhere infrastructure) is itself evidence that mainstream libm implementations do not correctly round clause-9.2 functions by default.

### Inferences
- Because clause 9.2 compliance (correct rounding of exp/log/sin/pow/etc.) is optional and essentially unimplemented in mainstream libraries, any claim of cross-library or cross-platform bit-exact reproducibility for code that calls these functions is almost certainly false unless the author has specifically pinned a single correctly-rounded library (e.g., CORE-MATH, RLIBM) on both sides of the comparison.
- The gap between "required, correctly rounded" (clause 5) and "recommended, usually not correctly rounded" (clause 9.2) is the single most important fact separating what is *guaranteed* by IEEE 754 from what is merely *common practice* in libm implementations — this is the fulcrum point a blog post should hinge its precision claims on.

### Gaps
- I did not independently verify the verbatim clause 9.2 text (e.g., the exact "shall return results correctly rounded..." sentence) against the primary standard PDF; this phrasing comes from AI-assisted search synthesis of the standard's content rather than a direct quote I pulled myself. Treat the *meaning* as solid (triple-corroborated across independent sources below) but the *exact wording* as unverified.

## Does IEEE 754 constrain evaluation order of a sequence of operations? Is floating-point addition associative?

### Takeaway
IEEE 754 defines the semantics of each individual operation (given its operands) but says nothing about how a compiler, language, or programmer must sequence or group a chain of several operations — that is a language/compiler concern, not a hardware-arithmetic concern. Floating-point addition (and more generally, summation) is not associative because each addition rounds its result to finite precision, so `(a+b)+c` and `a+(b+c)` can differ when intermediate rounding differs.

### Cited Findings
- Goldberg's classic example of non-associativity: "the expression (x+y)+z has a totally different answer than x+(y+z) when x = 10^30, y = -10^30 and z = 1 (it is 1 in the former case, 0 in the latter)." — David Goldberg, ["What Every Computer Scientist Should Know About Floating-Point Arithmetic"](https://docs.oracle.com/cd/E19957-01/806-3568/ncg_goldberg.html), ACM Computing Surveys 23(1), 1991, accessed 2026-10-14. (Mirror also available at the [UIowa PDF](http://homepage.divms.uiowa.edu/~atkinson/m170.dir/goldberg.pdf).)
- "Floating-point operations, as defined in the IEEE-754 standard, are not associative... the non-associativity is because of the rounding that may occur in finite-precision floating-point addition. For example, on a binary machine with two bits of precision, ((10+0.1)+(-0.1)) ≈ 11, whereas (10 + (0.1 + -0.1)) = 10." — search synthesis of multiple sources including [Effects of Floating-Point non-Associativity on Numerical Computations (Utah course notes)](https://www.sci.utah.edu/~beiwang/teaching/cs6210-fall-2016/nonassociativity.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14
- The IEEE background document on cross-implementation differences does *not* itself make an explicit statement about associativity or evaluation order of multi-operation sequences — I checked specifically and found no clause text addressing this in the material retrieved. — [Differences Among IEEE 754 Implementations](https://grouper.ieee.org/groups/msc/ANSI_IEEE-Std-754-2019/background/addendum.html), accessed 2026-10-14 (absence noted, not a positive claim)
- Indirectly corroborating that order-of-evaluation is left to languages/compilers rather than the arithmetic standard: GCC's and Clang's `-fassociative-math` / `-freciprocal-math` / `-funsafe-math-optimizations` flags exist specifically because, by default (without these flags), the compiler must *not* reorder floating-point operations — i.e., the C/C++ language standards' default sequencing rules (not IEEE 754) are what currently pin down evaluation order, and compilers only break this default sequencing when explicitly told to via "unsafe" math flags. See compiler-flags section below for direct quotes.

### Inferences
- The standard's silence on multi-operation sequencing is itself an important, precisely statable fact: IEEE 754 standardizes the *operation*, not the *program*. A "sequence of IEEE-754-conformant operations" is only reproducible if every detail outside of clause 5/9.2 — operation order, grouping/parentheses, contraction into FMA, and intermediate precision — is also pinned down by something **other than** IEEE 754 (i.e., by the language standard's evaluation rules and by specific, disciplined compiler flags).

### Gaps
- I could not find a sentence in IEEE 754-2019 itself (or an authoritative secondary source quoting it) that explicitly says "this standard does not constrain the order of evaluation of a sequence of operations." This appears to be a true and widely-accepted *inference* from the standard's scope (it only defines individual operations in clause 5 and clause 9.2, never multi-step expressions or reductions), but I did not find a single citable sentence making this absence explicit as a *positive statement in the standard's own text*. A blog author should phrase this as "the standard is silent on this; it does not appear anywhere in its normative scope," rather than quoting a specific disclaiming clause.

## How much do real libm implementations differ for exp/log/sin/pow? (glibc, musl, Apple, Intel SVML)

### Takeaway
Concrete, measured ULP (units-in-the-last-place) differences between libm implementations and even between architectures *within the same libm* are real and documented, though the level of official documentation varies a lot by vendor: glibc historically published a detailed per-architecture ULP table (now removed from the latest online manual in favor of an external academic paper restricted to x86_64); musl documents accuracy goals (≤1.5 ULP, with an explicit list of functions that ARE exactly/correctly rounded); Intel documents MKL VML accuracy tiers explicitly (HA <1.0 ULP, LA ≤4.0 ULP) and SVML is understood (via community sources, not an Intel ULP table I could locate) to target ~4 ULP worst case; Apple does not appear to publish a ULP accuracy table for Accelerate/vecLib at all.

### Cited Findings

**glibc**
- Current/latest glibc manual general accuracy-goals text (direct quote, fetched and parsed from the raw HTML of the live manual page): "Errors are measured in 'units of the last place'... Ideally the error for all functions is always less than 0.5ulps in round-to-nearest mode... Except for certain functions such as `sqrt`, `fma` and `rint` whose results are fully specified by reference to corresponding IEEE 754 floating-point operations, and conversions between strings and floating point, the GNU C Library does not aim for correctly rounded results for functions in the math library, and does not aim for correctness in whether 'inexact' exceptions are raised... Each function with a floating-point result behaves as if it computes an infinite-precision result that is within a few ulp... of the mathematically correct value... The math testsuite only flags results larger than 9ulp (or 16 for IBM `long double` format) as errors; although most of the implementations show errors smaller than the limit." — [GNU C Library manual, "Errors in Math Functions"](https://sourceware.org/glibc/manual/latest/html_node/Errors-in-Math-Functions.html), accessed 2026-10-14 (fetched and parsed directly from raw HTML in this session)
- The *current* glibc manual has **removed** the historical per-architecture numeric ULP table and instead refers readers to an external paper: "A more comprehensive analysis of the GNU C Library math functions precision could be found in 'Accuracy of Mathematical Functions in Single, Double, Double Extended, and Quadruple Precision'; Brian Gladman, Vincenzo Innocente, John Mather, and Paul Zimmermann at https://inria.hal.science/hal-03141101. It does not cover complex functions, nor jn/yn, and it is only for x86_64, and for rounding to nearest, and does not cover any architecture variations." — [GNU C Library manual, "Errors in Math Functions" (latest)](https://sourceware.org/glibc/manual/latest/html_node/Errors-in-Math-Functions.html), accessed 2026-10-14. **This is a notable finding for the blog post**: the "documented table of known maximum errors per function per architecture" that the research brief asked about existed in older glibc manuals but is not in the current (2026) online manual.
- The older glibc 2.27 manual (archived, still live at sourceware.org) *does* contain the full per-architecture table referenced in the brief. I fetched its raw HTML directly and parsed it programmatically (not via AI summary) to confirm concrete numbers. The table is split into 5 sub-tables, each covering a different architecture group, with 233 function rows: Table 1 = {AArch64, ARM, Alpha, ColdFire, Generic}; Table 2 = {HPPA, IA64, M68k, MIPS 32-bit, MIPS 64-bit}; Table 3 = {MicroBlaze, Nios II, PowerPC, PowerPC soft-float, RISC-V}; Table 4 = {RISC-V soft-float, S/390, SH, Sparc, Tile}; Table 5 = {i686, ix86, x86_64}. — [GNU C Library 2.27 manual, "Errors in Math Functions"](https://sourceware.org/glibc/manual/2.27/html_node/Errors-in-Math-Functions.html), accessed 2026-10-14

## What compiler flags change floating-point results, and what do they do?

### Takeaway
`-ffast-math`/`-Ofast` (GCC/Clang) relax multiple IEEE-754-adjacent guarantees simultaneously (NaN/Inf assumptions, reassociation, rounding mode assumptions, excess precision), FP contraction (FMA fusion) changes results by rounding a multiply-add once instead of twice, and legacy x87 80-bit excess precision historically caused unpredictable, spill-dependent result changes. All of these are compiler/ABI/language-level choices layered on top of IEEE 754, not something IEEE 754 itself controls.

### Cited Findings
- `-Ofast`: "Disregard strict standards compliance. `-Ofast` enables all `-O3` optimizations. It also enables optimizations that are not valid for all standard-compliant programs. It turns on `-ffast-math`, `-fallow-store-data-races`..." — [GCC documentation, Optimize Options](https://gcc.gnu.org/onlinedocs/gcc/Optimize-Options.html), accessed 2026-10-14
- `-ffast-math` (GCC) is documented (per search of the official GCC docs) to enable: `-fno-math-errno`, `-funsafe-math-optimizations`, `-ffinite-math-only`, `-fno-rounding-math`, `-fno-signaling-nans`, `-fcx-limited-range`, and `-fexcess-precision=fast`. — [GCC documentation, Optimize Options](https://gcc.gnu.org/onlinedocs/gcc/Optimize-Options.html), accessed 2026-10-14 (confirmed via search synthesis of the live doc; I was not able to pull the verbatim paragraph via direct fetch in this session, see Gaps)
- `-ffp-contract` (GCC): "`-ffp-contract=off` disables floating-point expression contraction. `-ffp-contract=fast` enables floating-point expression contraction such as forming of fused multiply-add operations if the target has native support for them. `-ffp-contract=on` enables floating-point expression contraction if allowed by the language standard." — [GCC documentation, Optimize Options](https://gcc.gnu.org/onlinedocs/gcc/Optimize-Options.html), accessed 2026-10-14 (directly fetched and quoted)
- Clang `-ffp-contract`: "specifies when the compiler is permitted to form fused floating-point operations, such as fused multiply-add (FMA)... available values are: {on, off, fast, fast-honor-pragmas}... Fused operations are permitted to produce more precise results than performing the same operations separately. Clang takes advantage of this by default (on)." — [Clang Compiler User's Manual](https://clang.llvm.org/docs/UsersManual.html), accessed 2026-10-14
- Clang `-ffp-model`: "`-ffp-model=[precise|fast|strict]`... **precise** (default): ...`-ffp-contract=on` and `-fno-fast-math`... **strict**: Disables floating-point contraction optimizations (`-ffp-contract=off`), honors dynamically-set floating-point rounding modes (`-frounding-math`), and disables all 'fast-math' floating-point optimizations (`-fno-fast-math`)... **fast**: Enables all 'fast-math' floating-point optimizations (`-ffast-math`) and enables floating-point contraction optimizations across C/C++ statements (`-ffp-contract=fast`)." — [Clang Compiler User's Manual](https://clang.llvm.org/docs/UsersManual.html), accessed 2026-10-14
- C standard `#pragma STDC FP_CONTRACT`: "contraction of expressions can be prevented with `#pragma STDC FP_CONTRACT OFF`. By default, fp_contract is on, which tells the compiler to use floating-point contraction instructions where possible." — search synthesis of the C99/C11 standard's pragma semantics, corroborated by [Microsoft Learn, fp_contract pragma](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/cpp/preprocessor/fp-contract?view=msvc-170), accessed 2026-10-14. Caveat on compiler conformance: "few compilers implement this pragma... as of 2007, gcc ignores the pragma with a warning... More recently, GCC ignores `#pragma STDC FP_CONTRACT OFF` and contracts `a + b * c` by default wherever the target has FMA." — search synthesis corroborated by discussion in [Floating-Point Contraction in C and Defensive Techniques](https://zenn.dev/mod_poppo/articles/c-fp-contract?locale=en), accessed 2026-10-14. **This is an important, concrete reproducibility hazard**: the standard-mandated way to turn off contraction (the pragma) has historically not been honored by at least GCC, meaning `-ffp-contract=off` (a compiler flag, not the pragma) is the more reliable lever in practice.
- x87 80-bit excess precision: "The x87 floating-point unit only supports internal computation with one data type: the 80-bit floating-point type... though this can be set to 32 or 64 bits using the FLDCW instruction." "C99 added `FLT_EVAL_METHOD`, which allows an implementer to evaluate the temporary values within expressions in higher precision instead of strictly sticking to the exact source types. However, at prescribed points in the program (assignment, parameter/return, explicit cast), the value must be truncated to its source type." "The actual semantics implemented by nonconforming compilers amounts to evaluating all float and double arithmetic in extended precision, except that at unpredictable points in time, it is truncated to the source precision... If code designed for x87 is ported to the lower-precision double-precision SSE2 floating point, certain combinations of math operations or input datasets can result in measurable numerical deviation... historically, language standards and compilers had been inconsistent in their handling of the x87 80-bit registers... compared with the double and single precision formats implemented in SSE2: the rounding of extended precision intermediate values to double precision variables was not fully defined and was dependent on implementation details such as when registers were spilled to memory." — search synthesis corroborated by [Wikipedia, X87](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X87), [Wikipedia, Extended precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_precision), and [Wikipedia, Long double](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_double), accessed 2026-10-14
- A concrete, empirically-demonstrated instance of excess-precision-driven non-reproducibility: Daniel Lemire's post "GNU GCC on x86 does not round floating-point divisions to the nearest value," demonstrating that 32-bit x86 GCC code (relying on x87) can produce a floating-point division result that is not the IEEE-754 correctly-rounded value for the target (single/double) precision, because the division was computed and rounded in 80-bit extended precision first. — [Lemire, "GNU GCC on x86 does not round floating-point divisions to the nearest value"](https://lemire.me/blog/2020/06/26/gcc-not-nearest/), accessed 2026-10-14
- The C++ standards committee is actively working (as of the 2024–2025 papers found) to tighten these semantics: "Floating-Point Excess Precision" [P3488R1] and "Tightening floating-point semantics for C++" [P3715R0] — [open-std.org P3488R1](https://open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2024/p3488r1.pdf), [open-std.org P3715R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2025/p3715r0.html), accessed 2026-10-14. This confirms that, as of 2026, excess-precision/contraction-driven non-reproducibility is still considered an open, unresolved problem at the C++ language-standard level, not something fully fixed by any current standard revision.
- Observed real-world effect on reproducibility: "tests show results are not always identical with `-ffast-math` enabled, whereas running the same program without `-ffast-math` produces identical results... `-ffast-math` does two types of things: it changes the code generation, and it mucks with the floating point settings at startup. On x86 systems with SSE2, this involves writing to the MXCSR register... fast-math allows the compiler to break some IEEE 754 rules, which may have significant downstream effects." — search synthesis of discussion including [Simon Byrne, "Beware of fast-math"](https://simonbyrne.github.io/notes/fastmath/), accessed 2026-10-14

### Inferences
- There are at least three independent compiler-level mechanisms, any one of which alone is sufficient to break bit-exact reproducibility even when every individual arithmetic operation is itself IEEE-754-conformant: (1) reassociation/unsafe-math flags reordering a chain of additions/multiplications (changing rounding at each step), (2) FP contraction fusing a multiply+add into a single-rounding FMA instead of two roundings, and (3) excess/extended precision (x87, or more generally any `FLT_EVAL_METHOD != 0` environment) carrying intermediate results at higher precision than the nominal type until an unpredictable spill/truncation point. A rigorous reproducibility claim must rule out all three, not just one.
- The FP_CONTRACT pragma's historical non-enforcement by GCC is a good concrete "standard vs. practice" gap to highlight in the article: the C standard *does* provide a portable, standardized way to request no-contraction, but relying on it has been unsafe in practice; the compiler-specific flag (`-ffp-contract=off`) has been the dependable lever.

## Reproducible/deterministic summation literature (Demmel & Nguyen, ReproBLAS, Kahan/Neumaier)

### Takeaway
There are two distinct but related bodies of work: (1) Kahan (1965) and Neumaier (1974) compensated-summation algorithms, which improve the *accuracy* of a sequential sum by tracking and re-adding rounding error, for a *fixed* summation order; and (2) Demmel, Nguyen, Ahrens et al.'s "reproducible summation"/ReproBLAS line of work (2013–2016), which solves a different problem — guaranteeing *bitwise-identical* results across different numbers of processors, different reduction tree shapes, and different scheduling (i.e., across different summation *orders*), which plain compensated summation does not guarantee by itself.

### Cited Findings
- Kahan's foundational compensated-summation paper: William Kahan, "Further remarks on reducing truncation errors," *Communications of the ACM*, vol. 8, issue 1, p. 40, 1965. — citation confirmed via search synthesis of [Wikipedia, Kahan summation algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahan_summation_algorithm), accessed 2026-10-14
- Neumaier's improved variant: A. Neumaier, "Rundungsfehleranalyse einiger Verfahren zur Summation endlicher Summen," *ZAMM*, vol. 54, pp. 39–51, 1974, yielding what is now called the Kahan–Babuška–Neumaier (KBN) algorithm, which improves on Kahan's method "for cases where the addend exceeds the running sum." — citation and description via search synthesis, corroborated by [A Generalized Kahan–Babuška Summation Algorithm (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00607-005-0139-x.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14
- Problem statement for reproducible summation (Demmel/Nguyen line): "computes bitwise identical results from multiple runs of the same program, addressing the challenge that dynamic scheduling of parallel computing resources combined with nonassociativity of floating point addition makes achieving reproducibility difficult." — search synthesis of [Demmel & Nguyen, "Fast Reproducible Floating-Point Summation" (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261025034_Fast_Reproducible_Floating-Point_Summation) and [Parallel Reproducible Summation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273572726_Parallel_Reproducible_Summation), accessed 2026-10-14
- Algorithmic approach: "The 'Pre-Rounding' algorithm for reproducible sum begins by computing the maximum absolute value of input numbers, then rounding all inputs to 1 ulp of that maximum value, after which the rounded numbers behave like fixed point numbers so summation is exact and reproducible... every core computes its partial sum as a triple of floating-point numbers aligned on boundaries of the form 2·W, where W is 40 for the binary64 format." — search synthesis of [Demmel, "Efficient Reproducible Floating Point Summation and BLAS," EECS-2016-121 tech report, UC Berkeley](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2016/EECS-2016-121.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14
- The resulting library: "ReproBLAS is available at http://bebop.cs.berkeley.edu/reproblas/." — [Peter Ahrens, Jim Demmel, Hong Diep Nguyen, "Reproducible BLAS" slides](https://www.netlib.org/utk/people/JackDongarra/WEB-PAGES/Batched-BLAS-2016/Day1/10_Demmel_ReproBLAS.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14; project also referenced as "RARE-BLAS (Reproducible, Accurately Rounded and Efficient BLAS)" in [Reproducible, Accurately Rounded and Efficient BLAS (Springer chapter)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-58943-5_49), accessed 2026-10-14
- A related, independent project solving the same problem differently: ExBLAS, "Reproducible and Accurate BLAS Library" — [ExBLAS paper (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/288828110_ExBLAS_Reproducible_and_Accurate_BLAS_Library), accessed 2026-10-14 (title/existence only; I did not extract its technical approach in this session)

### Inferences
- The existence of an entire specialized sub-field (reproducible summation / ReproBLAS / ExBLAS, roughly 2013–2017 with ongoing interest) is itself strong indirect evidence for the article's core reproducibility thesis: if bitwise reproducibility across parallel reduction orders were already guaranteed by IEEE 754 or by ordinary compensated summation, this research area would not need to exist. The *problem being solved* (dynamic scheduling + floating-point non-associativity defeats naive reproducibility) is the precise, citable articulation of "why reproducible summation is needed."
- Kahan/Neumaier compensated summation and Demmel/Nguyen-style reproducible summation should not be conflated in the blog post: compensated summation improves accuracy for one fixed order; reproducible summation targets identical bits across different, possibly-parallel orders (often at some accuracy or performance cost, e.g., the "pre-rounding"/fixed-point-like technique described above).

## Is there an authoritative statement that two IEEE-754-conformant implementations, given identical inputs and identical operation sequences, must produce identical results? Under what precise conditions IS bit-exactness guaranteed?

### Takeaway
There is an explicit, authoritative statement that the standard does **not** guarantee bit-identical results for a *program* across conformant systems. Bit-exactness IS strictly guaranteed only at the level of a *single* clause-5 required operation: given identical input bit patterns (same format), the same rounding-direction attribute, and the same required operation (+, −, ×, ÷, √, FMA, remainder, or a conversion), every IEEE-754-2019-conformant implementation must produce the identical output bit pattern, because "correctly rounded" is defined as "round the unique, exact mathematical result" — leaving zero implementation freedom. Extending that guarantee to a multi-step program additionally requires fixing everything IEEE 754 itself is silent on: operation order/grouping, FMA contraction, intermediate/excess precision, and — if any clause-9.2 recommended function (exp, log, sin, pow, …) is used — the specific library implementing it.

### Cited Findings
- Direct, authoritative statement that program-level bit-exactness is **not** guaranteed: "the IEEE standard does not guarantee that the same program will deliver identical results on all conforming systems." — [IEEE 754 background document, "Differences Among IEEE 754 Implementations"](https://grouper.ieee.org/groups/msc/ANSI_IEEE-Std-754-2019/background/addendum.html), accessed 2026-10-14. This same document explicitly pushes back on an informal stronger claim sometimes attributed to Goldberg/Kahan-style folk wisdom — that "if any intermediate result differs [when a program is moved between two IEEE-conformant machines], it must be because of software bugs" — characterizing that stronger claim as *not* actually guaranteed by the standard.
- The standard's correct-rounding definition for required operations (restated from the first question above, directly relevant here): "every operation shall be performed as if it first produced an intermediate result correct to infinite precision and with unbounded range, and then rounded that result according to one of the attributes [the rounding-direction attributes] in [the standard]." — search synthesis of [IEEE Standard 754-2019 PDF](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~vinals/tspot_files/phys4041/2020/IEEE%20Standard%20754-2019.pdf), accessed 2026-10-14. Because the "infinite precision" mathematical result of a fully-specified operation on given finite bit-pattern operands is a unique real number (or unique special value), and the rounding function for a given rounding-direction attribute and destination format is a unique, deterministic function of that real number, there is no remaining degree of freedom for *this one operation* — any two conformant implementations must agree bit-for-bit on it.
- Corroborating, independently-sourced explanation of *why* a full program is not covered by this guarantee (synthesizing points established in other sections of these notes, each individually cited there): (a) clause 9.2 "recommended" functions are not required to be correctly rounded and in practice differ library-to-library (see the libm-differences section above); (b) the standard does not constrain multi-operation evaluation order, so compiler/language choices (reassociation flags, FP contraction, excess precision) determine whether a "sequence of operations" is even the *same* sequence of rounded steps on two systems (see the compiler-flags section above); (c) floating-point addition is non-associative, so a different grouping of the same operations is not guaranteed to agree even in exact arithmetic terms, let alone bit-for-bit (see Goldberg citation above).

### Inferences
- Precise statement of the conditions under which bit-exactness IS guaranteed by IEEE 754 alone, synthesized from the cited clauses/sources above (this is my own synthesis built strictly from the cited material, not a single verbatim quote from one source): bit-exact, cross-implementation reproducibility is guaranteed **only** for a single invocation of a clause-5 required operation (add, subtract, multiply, divide, squareRoot, fusedMultiplyAdd, remainder, or a format conversion), when (i) the input operand bit patterns and formats are identical, (ii) the applicable rounding-direction attribute (and other relevant attributes, e.g. no signaling-NaN/ trap differences affecting the numeric result) is identical, and (iii) the destination format is identical. To extend any such guarantee to a *sequence* of operations (a real program), one must additionally and independently fix, by means outside IEEE 754 itself: the exact order/grouping of operations (no reassociation), whether/when multiply-add pairs are contracted into FMA (no silent contraction, or contraction applied identically and deterministically on both sides), the absence of any excess/extended intermediate precision (e.g. consistent `FLT_EVAL_METHOD`, no x87 80-bit spill-dependent truncation), and — for any clause-9.2 recommended operation used (exp, log, sin, cos, pow, etc.) — use of the bit-for-bit identical implementation (same library, same version, same build flags) on both sides, since the standard does not pin these down at all.
- This framing directly answers the brief's core ask: "the standard requires" = clause 5 operations, correctly rounded, single operation, fixed inputs/format/rounding attribute; "the standard recommends" = clause 9.2 operations, correct rounding only as an optional target, essentially unmet in practice; "implementations happen to do" = everything about multi-step program reproducibility (evaluation order, contraction, excess precision, specific libm choice), none of which IEEE 754 addresses at all, and which is instead governed (partially, and imperfectly) by language standards (C/C++ evaluation and contraction pragmas), compiler flags, and specialized reproducible-computation libraries (ReproBLAS, etc.).
